# Writing about identifiable people

*One of the org's [publication standards](README.md).*

**Status:** v1.0 (2026-08-05). Org-wide. Binds **every document in every repo** in the
[social-network-health](https://github.com/social-network-health) organization, and every surface
the org publishes on — research documents, the toolkit wiki, and video alike.

*If you or someone you know is struggling: in the US, call or text **988** (Suicide & Crisis
Lifeline); elsewhere, https://findahelpline.com.*

---

## Why this exists

Social network health research cannot avoid studying harm to real people. Erasing the subject is
not an option — the parity principle in
[`memoriams.md`](../../research/research_library/memoriams.md) is an argument *against* erasure, and a
threat model with no evidence behind it is worthless. The question was never whether to study this.
It is how to present it.

We wrote presentation norms once, applied them to one document, and discovered later that a sibling
document citing the same underlying material had drifted to the opposite posture. **A posture that
binds one file is not a standard.** This document binds all of them.

## Scope

Applies to any document, in any org repo, that references an identifiable individual — including a
person identifiable *indirectly*, by combination of details, or through a source it cites.

"Identifiable" is not limited to names. It includes online handles and pseudonyms, which
[BPS 2021](https://cms.bps.org.uk/sites/default/files/2022-06/Ethics%20Guidelines%20for%20Internet-mediated%20Research_0.pdf)
says should be *"treated with the same respect as a researcher would treat a participant's real
name,"* and which EU law treats as personal data outright (GDPR Art. 4(1), "online identifier").

# Part A — Presentation rules

**These apply always.** They are not tiered, not discounted by how long a source has been public,
and not subject to case-by-case exception. Part B decides whether you may publish at all; Part A
decides how, and it applies at every tier.

## A1. Identify the parties separately

A single case usually involves three parties with different claims. Decide each one on its own;
collapsing them is what produced our first drift.

| Party | Claim | Governing idea |
|---|---|---|
| **The subject** (living or deceased) | Strongest where the material is sensitive and they never chose publication | Deceased subjects hold **limited conferred rights** — the productive frame, per [Caswell & Turner 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/1747016120952503), rather than arguing whether the dead can be harmed. [NESH Guideline 29](https://www.forskningsetikk.no/en/guidelines/social-sciences-and-humanities/guidelines-for-research-ethics-in-the-social-sciences-and-the-humanities/): research on the deceased must meet *"the same standards for respect, documentation, and accountability as research involving living people."* |
| **The author of a source** we cite | Weakest — they chose to publish | Ordinary scholarly citation. Authorship and credit norms apply, not confidentiality. |
| **Surviving family, and third parties in the material** | Strong, and independent of the other two | They consented to nothing. [Bak & Willems 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9349167/): such material is *constitutively about living third parties who were never subjects.* |

Quoting an author's **argument** and republishing a **subject's circumstances** are different acts,
even when both appear in the same source. Separate them before you write.

## A2. Cause discipline

**Never assert a cause of death the public record has not established, and never assert more than
your source does.**

Two tiers, applied everywhere:

- **Confirmed** — an official ruling, or a family's public statement in reliable sources.
- **Cause not public** — publicly mourned, cause never publicly established. **Never asserted
  otherwise, in any document, at any tier.**

A third party's stated *belief* about a cause — including a family member's, an employer's, or a
project leader's — is **not** a determination. [Samaritans](https://www.samaritans.org/about-samaritans/media-guidelines/):
*"Even if a family member attributes the suicide to something specific, remember not to over-simplify
the causes… It's safer to leave the determination of cause of death to a coroner."*

This is the best-evidenced rule in the entire corpus we surveyed. The
[#chatsafe 2.0 Delphi](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0289494) study's highest-consensus item,
at 100%, is *"post only what they know to be true"* — and its expert panel declined to endorse even
carefully-hedged causal speculation.

Do not attribute a death to a single cause. WHO: *"It is inaccurate to imply that one factor alone
causes a person to take his or her own life."*

## A3. Generalise personal detail

The rule the guidelines actually state is **generalisation, not anonymisation**, and it exists for a
reason distinct from privacy: personal detail drives **over-identification**, which is the mechanism
of contagion. [Reporting on Suicide](https://www.save.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ROS-One-Pager-2024.pdf):
*"Keep information about the person general. Why? Sharing personal information can lead some to
over-identify with the person who died."*

The same detail also drives re-identification, by a second and independent route. **Age + role +
organisation + date is the canonical "jigsaw identification" set.** Publishing three of the four
usually identifies the fourth.

So: carry the detail your analysis actually needs, and no more. If a detail is not doing analytic
work, it is doing identifying work.

## A4. Quotation

Distinguish two cases:

- **An author's normative or analytical claim** — their argument, in their words, about what should
  change. Quote it and attribute it. This is ordinary scholarship.
- **Description of an identifiable person's death, distress, or private circumstances** — do not
  reproduce verbatim, even from a public source, and even when the source is the person's own
  community.

Paraphrase does **not** rescue the second case. [Ayers et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6550214/)
measured re-identification of quoted material at **84%**;
[Reagle 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9463657/) located the sources of all verbatim
quotes tested and **11 of 19 reworded** ones. Do not assume a rewrite protects anyone.

**Test searchability against archives, not Google.** Reagle found archive search outperformed web
search roughly 4:1. A quote that returns nothing in a search engine may still be one query away in an
archive. *"The best disguise is tested disguise."*

**Public availability does not license republication.** WHO states the principle plainly: *"Even if
such texts may be in the public domain, the audience for an online/print article or broadcast piece
will be different from the original viewers or recipients of the information."* Republishing moves
material to an audience that did not choose it. That is the harm; it does not diminish with the age
of the source.

## A5. Safe messaging applies to our own artifacts

Not only to what we say about our work publicly — to the documents themselves. Per
[Mindframe's researcher guidance](https://cdn.mindframe.org.au/assets/src/uploads/OurWordsMatter_Quick_Ref_Researchers_Final_FF.pdf),
this covers **titles, headings, abstracts and body text**.

- Say "died by suicide" / "took their own life." Never "committed" (implies criminality), never
  "successful," "failed," or "completed."
- **Never describe method.** No exceptions, no tiers.
- Do not name locations as sites.
- No glamorising, sanctifying, or romanticising — including uncritical reproduction of tributes and
  memorial language.
- Avoid framing a death as **inexplicable given the person's capability or achievement**. The
  [Action Alliance](https://suicidepreventionmessaging.org/safety) warns that *"describing suicidal
  behaviour as the inexplicable act of an otherwise healthy or high-achieving person may encourage
  identification with the person who died."* This constrains how we frame findings, not just how we
  report deaths.
- Any document discussing a suicide death carries crisis resources.

## A6. Citation transparency

**Never silently omit a citation, a quote, or a detail for protective reasons.** State that
something has been withheld or generalised, what class of thing it is, and why.

This is not pedantry. Silent omission is a documented cover for fabrication —
[Dougherty 2021](https://philarchive.org/archive/DOUTUO-4) found a published article in which every
quotation attributed to anonymised interviewees was traceable verbatim to prior scholarship. A reader
who cannot see that we withheld something cannot distinguish our redaction from that.

Where alteration is used, it must not change what the evidence shows.
[ICMJE](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/protection-of-research-participants.html):
deidentifying changes must *"not distort scientific meaning."* Undisclosed alteration that changes
the finding is falsification, not protection.

## A7. Keep verifiability, move it if you must

An evidence-linked claim must stay checkable. When the evidence cannot be published as-is, **relocate
verification rather than deleting it**: a public, claim-level note describing the evidence and its
nature, with the material itself held privately.

This is the [ATI / Qualitative Data Repository](https://qdr.syr.edu/ati/why-ati) model —
*"as open as possible, as closed as necessary."* We already have the architecture for it: an
anonymised public summary over private case records.

# Part B — The release gate

Part A governs presentation. Part B governs **permission**.

## B1. When a release is required

**Where personally identifiable information is likely to be linked back to an individual — directly
or indirectly — a release is required.**

That is our standard, and it is deliberately stricter than what most publishers require. It reflects
what the people involved actually expect: asked about research use of their public posts, **around
80%** expected to be asked for consent, and **over 90%** wanted to remain anonymous
([Williams et al. 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0038038517708140)).

No release is required for genuinely aggregate or de-identified findings that survive A3.

## B2. Blocking or deferred

The requirement is constant. **What varies is whether we must hold the release before we publish.**

**BLOCKING — obtain the release first. Do not publish while waiting.**

- The material has **not already been public**. We are never the first to disclose.
- The publication is **irreversible** — a delivered talk, a released video, a printed proceeding, a
  distributed PDF, anything we cannot retract or amend after the fact.
- The subject is a **private individual** rather than the author of a public work.
- The material is **sensitive and specific** to an identifiable living person.
- **Always blocking regardless of tier:** method detail, and any cause assertion beyond A2.

**DEFERRED — publish under Part A, seek the release in parallel.**

Available only when **all** of these hold:

1. The material is **already public**, and has been for long enough that we are adding reach rather
   than making a disclosure.
2. Our use is **reversible** — we can edit, generalise further, or withdraw it on request, and the
   artifact is one we control.
3. Part A has been **fully applied first** — generalised, cause-disciplined, no death-descriptive
   quotation. The deferred tier is not a reason to publish more.
4. The obligation is **recorded and owned** (see B3).

**Reversibility is what licenses the deferred tier — not publicness.** "It has been public for years"
is an argument about identification risk, and a fair one. It is not an argument about contagion,
because our readers are a different audience who have not seen it. Age discounts B, never A.

## B3. Tracking a deferred release

A deferred release that is not tracked is not deferred, it is skipped. Each one gets:

- **An issue in the private repo** (`snh-private`), never a public one. A public issue reading "we owe
  a release for X" is itself a pointer to X, and would undo the protection Part A just bought.
- **A named owner** and a **review date**.
- **A defined fallback if the release is refused or cannot be obtained**, decided *before* publishing.
  Because the tier requires reversibility, this is always available: generalise further, or withdraw.

If we would not be willing to act on a refusal, the item was never eligible for the deferred tier.

## B4. The full release process

Our existing practice for produced material — a signed release before recording, a review copy of the
finished piece, and changes made at the subject's request before publication — is the top rung of this
ladder, not a separate world. Use it wherever the work is planned, the subject is reachable, and the
timeline can carry it.

It is slow by design, and that is correct for what it covers. Part B exists so that its slowness does
not become the reason a whole research programme stalls, not to provide a way around it.

# Part C — Honest gaps

Where no standard exists, we say so and record our choice as a decision rather than dressing it as a
discovered rule.

1. **Quoting the text of a memoriam is ungoverned.** No source we surveyed addresses it — not WHO,
   Reporting on Suicide, Samaritans, Mindframe, COPE, ICMJE, AoIR, NESH, or any publisher. #chatsafe
   put memorial-page questions to an expert panel and reached **no consensus in either direction**.
   Our A4 line is our own construction.
2. **Naming versus anonymising a decedent in scholarly work is ungoverned.** Publisher policies are
   written around patients and research participants. ICMJE's recommendations contain no occurrence
   of "deceased," "decedent," or "next of kin."
3. **No scholarly policy sets an evidentiary standard for asserting a cause of death.** A2 imports a
   journalism norm because the scholarly world does not supply one.
4. **Whether contacting bereaved family protects or harms is genuinely contested.** NESH makes
   dialogue the default; the bereavement literature warns that contact re-inflicts loss. We have not
   resolved this and do not pretend to.
5. **There is no proxy-consent doctrine for a decedent's online material**, and the legal next of kin
   is often not who the person would have chosen.
6. **Guideline threat models assume string matching.** None accounts for archive search, semantic
   search, or stylometric attribution. A4 is written against a threat model the guidelines have not
   caught up with.
7. **The evidence base is thinner than the guidelines imply.** A systematic comparison of 24
   guidelines found *"no recommendations were in all guidelines"* and that none of 44 research papers
   supplied empirical support for any specific recommendation
   ([Mishara & Dargis 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2018.09.031)). We follow consensus here,
   and consensus is not measurement.

## Applying this

- **Any document citing case material is bound by Part A.** If you are adding such a citation, this
  standard applies to your change.
- **Case records stay private.** The anonymised public summary plus private named records
  architecture is unchanged and remains the org's posture.
- **Raising a concern is never wrong here.** If applying this feels like it requires naming a case,
  stop and ask rather than proceeding.

Sourcing for every claim above is held in the org's private research staging area. The underlying
postvention norms, with their defining sources, are in
[`memoriams.md`](../../research/research_library/memoriams.md).
